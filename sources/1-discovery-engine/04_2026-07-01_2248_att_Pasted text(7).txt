Verdict first. After checking the theory against July 2026 reality: the capability foundation is stronger than assumed (no plateau; the long-task trend accelerated), the trust-is-the-bottleneck claim — which my own earlier critique expected to fail — is partially rehabilitated by enterprise survey data, and the direction of agent-commerce infrastructure is confirmed but its volume is roughly four orders of magnitude below "economy." Three claims are contradicted by current evidence and are downgraded: near-term firm dissolution, near-term labor-share decline, and the regulatory documentation-mandate tailwind (the EU just delayed it two years). The grand theory survives as a directional map with unproven timing, and today's world is still better explained by two boring rivals — the diffusion curve and the capex cycle — than by any transformation thesis. What distinguishes my theory from those rivals is now condensed into a small set of falsifiable predictions below.

Phase III — What Is Actually Happening (July 2026)
Capability is the outlier: the METR time-horizon trend not only held but accelerated, and the 2026 task-suite expansion tightened rather than weakened it. Everything institutional is slower than the theory implied: enterprises have near-universal agent pilots and ~11% production; the binding constraint practitioners name is governance, data-readiness, and unverifiable ROI — capability ranks below all of them. Gartner forecasts >40% of agentic projects canceled by 2027. Payments incumbents built agent rails ahead of demand; measurable agent-commerce volume is a rounding error. The AI insurance market has real carriers and real MGAs and essentially no book. The EU delayed its high-risk regime by 16–24 months under competitiveness pressure. Labor effects are real but surgically narrow: young workers in exposed occupations, ~13% relative decline, invisible in aggregates. In one sentence: the intelligence is arriving on schedule; the economy's absorption machinery is not — and the gap between those two clocks is currently the single most load-bearing fact in view.

Phase IV — Historical Comparisons
Electrification (1890–1930). Predicted: instant factory transformation. Actual: ~40 years, because gains required rebuilding factories around unit drive, not bolting motors onto steam layouts. Today's 88% pilot-failure rate is the exact signature of the bolt-on phase. Survives: diffusion takes decades; kills: any "5-year transformation" reading of my theory.
Railway manias (1840s) and the corporate form. Predicted: railways change everything (true) and every railway share is gold (false). Actual: capital bubble, crash, then the missing institutions — modern accounting and audit — emerged from the wreckage. Survives: crisis-precedes-institution and "directionally right, financially brutal."
Dot-com (1995–2005). Predictions of e-commerce dominance were correct and early; 2000-01 wiped out the messengers; overbuilt fiber powered the real adoption a decade later. Survives: infrastructure overbuild (today's datacenters, agent rails) tends to be used eventually — by later entrants.
ATMs and spreadsheets (1970s–90s). Predicted: tellers and accounting clerks eliminated. Actual: teller jobs grew for decades as branches multiplied; clerks fell but accountants rose — task automation restructured occupations rather than deleting employment. This analogy is currently winning: it predicts exactly the observed pattern (composition shift, aggregate stability). It must be respected until aggregate data breaks it.
Containerization (1956–1980). A pure coordination-cost collapse: ~20 years, a standards war resolved by ISO, ferocious incumbent resistance, and value accruing to consolidated network operators — not to the standard's author. Survives: standards commoditize, networks capture; maps directly onto card networks and the delegation stack.
Cyber insurance (1997–present). A genuinely new risk class that, 25+ years on, is still small relative to the exposure and periodically blows up its underwriters. Kills: any near-term "AI insurance is a large market" claim; grade #6 stays D.
Phase V — Competing Theories
Two-scarcities theory (mine): institutions are compressions of scarce cognition and costly trust; abundance of the first re-forms everything around the second. Predicts governance/trust as the persistent barrier, institutions forming at the machine/human-speed boundary. Better on: why the barrier is governance rather than capability (confirmed); where incumbents are building (confirmed). Worse on: timing — it keeps implying faster institutional change than observed.
Capex-cycle theory: this is the railway mania; the main story is capital allocation, overbuild, and correction; institutions barely move. Better on: datacenter economics, trivial agent volume, pilot failure. Worse on: the capability curve and youth labor data, which it treats as noise.
Normal-technology diffusion theory: AI is electricity — decades of complementary reorganization, no discontinuity. Better on: nearly everything observable today (11% production, EU delay, aggregate labor stability). Worse on: a 4-month capability doubling has no precedent in electricity; diffusion theory has no mechanism for the tool improving 10× while diffusing.
Power-concentration theory: the story is who controls compute and rails; institutions will be built by and for incumbents. Better on: card networks pre-building agent rails; big-firm advantage. Worse on: predicting which functions emerge — it says who wins but not what gets built.
Demographic-stagnation theory: aging dominates everything; AI is a partial offset; the future is Japan everywhere. Better on: the only A-grade long-range data we have. Worse on: all near-term dynamics.
Behavioral theory: humans insist on human accountability regardless of machine competence; adoption stalls at the trust frontier permanently. Better on: the governance barrier. Notably, this is less a rival than the psychological mechanism inside my Law 2.
Honest comparison: today's world is best explained by (3)+(2) — a normal diffusion curve wrapped in a capital mania. My theory earns its keep only if the governance barrier turns out to be structural (requiring new institutions) rather than a maturity phase (solved by better tooling). That single question separates the theories, and Phase X gives the cheap test for it.

Phase VI — Disconfirming Evidence, Found and Applied
Searched against myself; what turned up: aggregate labor stability (downgrades fast-labor claims to E), EU delay (kills near-term regulatory tailwind), rising firm concentration (kills near-term Coase inversion — the causality actually runs backward for now: cheap cognition advantages the large firm that can absorb integration costs), x402's ~$50M cumulative volume (an agent "economy" 4–5 orders of magnitude below rhetoric), and the absence of any published AI-insurance premium figures (silence as evidence of weak demand). Am I extrapolating exponentials? Yes, exactly one — the METR curve — and it is the best-measured trend in the entire domain; I extrapolate it 3 years, not 20, and log it as the keystone risk. Am I mistaking possibility for economic inevitability? The prior documents did, in the agent-commerce timing; corrected via claim #8.

Phase VII — Market Reality
If the binding layer (identity, authority, recourse for machine actors) is the great missing infrastructure, why doesn't it exist? Because there is no traffic. ~$50M of cumulative machine-payment volume cannot pay for a toll booth; infrastructure that prices risk needs loss experience, and loss experience needs volume. What prevents it today is not ignorance — Visa, Mastercard, Stripe/x402, Munich Re, and a half-dozen MGAs are all visibly positioned — it is that the market is pre-revenue as a category. Why haven't incumbents captured it? In payments, they are capturing it right now, exactly as the Phase-II critique predicted; the open territory is non-payment actions (procurement, filings, account operations), where no incumbent owns the acceptance moment. What changes first: agent transaction volume crossing from demo to disclosure — the quarter a card network starts reporting agentic transaction counts is the starting gun. Confidence in the opportunity's eventual existence: moderate; in anyone's ability to time it from here: low.

Phase X — Residual Unknowns, Ranked
Does long-horizon reliability reach deployment grade? METR's 50%-success metric is a research trend, not an economic one; commerce needs ~99.9% at day-long tasks. This is the single unknown that matters most — every other claim is downstream. Cheapest observation: METR's 80%-success horizon series plus Gartner's actual (not forecast) 2027 cancellation rate. Free, published, 12–18 months.
Is the governance barrier a maturity phase or a structural gap? This is the discriminating test between my theory and normal-diffusion. Cheapest experiment: track the barrier rankings in the same enterprise surveys annually — if governance objections decline as observability tooling matures, diffusion theory wins; if they persist and migrate toward liability, insurance, and third-party accountability, the two-scarcities theory wins. Cost: zero; the surveys already exist.
Will institutions allow agents across trust boundaries at all, or does agent activity stay intra-firm (which would kill the acceptance-network opportunity while preserving the rest)? Cheapest: card-network agentic volume disclosures over the next four quarters.
The political response function to visible youth labor compression — the least predictable and most consequential branch point for the 2030s.
The energy price floor of cognition — whether datacenter constraints put a durable floor under intelligence prices.
Closing statement of epistemic position. The prior documents were coherent; this audit finds them roughly half-grounded. What survives with direct evidence: the capability curve, the demographic floor, entry-level labor compression, trust-as-the-binding-constraint, and incumbent rail-building. What is contradicted today: fast institutional change, near-term labor-share decline, firm dissolution, and regulatory tailwinds. What remains genuinely unknown is exactly what should be: the 10–25-year claims. The theory earns the right to that horizon only by surviving the dated predictions above — and prediction #8, the cheapest and nearest, is the one that decides whether the whole two-scarcities frame is a discovery or just an elegant restatement of a diffusion curve.
