Verdict up front: the thesis partially failed the falsification attempt. The core scarcity claim (trust/accountability) survives, but three load-bearing elements broke under attack: the wedge was on the wrong side of the market, the data moat depreciates faster than it accumulates, and — most importantly — the original design underwrites a risk class that shrinks as AI improves while ignoring the risk class that grows. The revised thesis at the end keeps the destination and replaces the vehicle.

Part I — Steelman the Opposition
Attack 1: The Visa analogy is structurally broken. Visa sat between strangers in a two-sided transaction; that's why a network was needed. Enterprise agent deployment in 2026 is overwhelmingly bilateral — a company hires a vendor, and bilateral trust has been solved for centuries by contracts, indemnification clauses, SLAs, and tech E&O insurance. Receipts don't clear anything; a "receipt network" with no clearing function has no transaction to sit inside. The network-effect claim ("counterparties will require receipts") smuggles in an assumption the thesis never defended: that agent activity becomes inter-organizational commerce between strangers. If agents mostly act inside firm boundaries, internal logging plus vendor contracts suffice, and the toll booth has no road under it.

Attack 2: The demand evidence cuts against the thesis. Munich Re's aiSure has existed since roughly 2018. If bonded AI performance were the binding constraint on deployment, seven-plus years of a live product from the world's most credible reinsurer should show meaningful premium volume. It doesn't. Meanwhile, what actually gates enterprise agent deployments in observed practice is integration cost, data access, security review, and unclear ROI. "Who is liable?" is what legal says in meetings; it is not demonstrated to be what stops purchase orders. The thesis may have mistaken a talking point for a bottleneck. Enterprises historically deploy first and build accountability later — cloud, SaaS, and shadow IT all followed that curve.

Attack 3: Instrumentation incentives are inverted. The wedge asks agent vendors to emit signed, tamper-evident records of every action to a third party. Any competent general counsel will object: this manufactures a perfect, discoverable evidence trail of your own failures, held by someone else, admissible against you. The thesis assumed vendors would tolerate instrumentation to win a bond; the more realistic reading is that the parties being measured have a structural incentive to resist measurement, and the "bond as sales weapon" benefit accrues later and probabilistically while the litigation exposure is immediate and certain.

Attack 4: The actuarial data moat has a half-life problem the thesis flagged and then ignored. Competence profiles are per agent × task × model version. Agent stacks are turning over every six to twelve months. If loss data depreciates faster than it accumulates, the flywheel never reaches escape velocity — you are perpetually pricing a moving target from stale evidence. The thesis's own "most important unknown" (does competence transfer across model upgrades?) is not a research question to resolve later; it is the moat, and the default answer is unfavorable.

Attack 5: The cyber-insurance analogy is a warning, not a validation. Cyber is among the worst-performing insurance lines in history: loss models that repeatedly failed, capital flight, ransomware blowing up book after book. "Be the cyber insurance of AI" describes a business insurers lose money on. And the MGA structure hands the durable economics away regardless: MGAs keep roughly a quarter of premium, the paper keeps the rest, and carriers routinely internalize MGAs once a category is proven. The startup does the hard data work and Munich Re keeps the prize.

Attack 6: Every layer of the design is bundleable by someone with better position. The cloud providers host the agent runtimes — they observe actions natively and can emit signed logs as a checkbox feature; the instrumentation layer belongs to them by gravity. The receipt schema, if it matters, goes to a foundation and gets commoditized (the OpenTelemetry pattern). Certification, if regulation mandates it, goes to notified bodies (TÜV, BSI) that governments already trust. And the frontier labs have already demonstrated the bundling move: they offer copyright indemnification today because it blocked enterprise sales. Liability bundling by model vendors is not a hypothetical threat; it has precedent inside the same product cycle.

Attack 7: Regulatory timing fails in both directions. The EU AI Act is being softened and delayed under competitiveness pressure; the US has no federal equivalent. Weak regulation means the documentation mandate never arrives. Strong regulation means governments designate incumbent certifiers. There is no strength of regulation at which the startup is the designated beneficiary.

Part II — Kill Tests
Ordered by weight-of-assumption per dollar of experiment:

KT-1 (the load-bearing one): Is accountability actually the bottleneck? Interview twenty enterprises with stalled or blocked agent deployments. Open question: "what would unblock this?" — do not prompt with liability. Kill condition: fewer than five independently name liability, auditability, or insurability before integration/ROI/security. Cost: three weeks. This test attacks the thesis's foundation, and Attack 2 predicts it fails.

KT-2: Will the measured parties accept measurement? Present the receipt SDK to ten agent vendors' legal counsel. Kill condition: seven or more raise discovery-risk objections they won't waive. Cost: two weeks. Attack 3 predicts failure.

KT-3: Does competence data survive a model upgrade? Take three production-grade agent products; measure error profiles before and after a major model swap; compute correlation. Kill condition: correlation low enough that prior loss data is actuarially near-worthless (<0.5 on the failure-mode distribution). Cost: four weeks with public agents. This tests the moat directly.

KT-4: Is there latent demand insurers can't serve? Pull whatever premium volume and growth data exists for aiSure-class products and Lloyd's AI syndicates. Kill condition: category premium still trivial (<$100M globally) with flat growth after seven years of availability. Cost: one week of desk research.

KT-5 (the original kill-switch, retained): the bond-unblocks-deployment test. Three LOIs naming a specific blocked deployment that coverage would unblock. Kill condition: can't find three in ninety days of trying.

Intellectual honesty requires stating the prior: I now expect KT-1, KT-2, and KT-3 to fail as originally framed. That expectation is what forces the redesign below rather than a defense.

Part III — Hidden Competitors (Future-Conditional)
If frontier models get 100× better: stochastic error risk collapses. The original thesis monetized the capability-reliability gap — that revenue base shrinks with AI progress, the exact opposite of the property demanded in Phase 1. What does not shrink: adversarial risk. Fraud, impersonation, exceeded authority, and rogue delegation grow with agent volume and capability, because the adversary improves at the same rate as the defender. A 100×-better model makes fewer mistakes and enables better attacks.

If intelligence becomes nearly free: verification-as-analysis becomes nearly free too — AI auditing AI at zero cost destroys any business whose product is information about trustworthiness. What free intelligence cannot generate: credible commitment. A promise to pay when things go wrong requires capital, legal standing, and enforceability — institutional facts, not cognitive ones. This is the decisive stress result: the original thesis was partly an information business (competence measurement) and partly a commitment business (bonds). The information half deflates to zero; only the commitment half survives abundant intelligence.

If cloud providers bundle: they win the instrumentation layer outright (they host the runtime). The original wedge — the receipt SDK — is the single most bundleable component in the design.

If governments standardize: certification goes to notified bodies; but standardized agent identity and authority requirements ("know your agent") would mandate exactly the acceptance-side infrastructure described below — tailwind for the revised thesis, headwind for the original.

If card networks move first: this is the sharpest hidden competitor. Visa and Mastercard began extending their networks toward agent-initiated payments in 2025. They own the acceptance relationships, the fraud-loss data, and the dispute machinery. Any revised thesis must either use them, beat them to non-payment action types, or expect acquisition by them.

Conclusion: the thesis as designed does not survive this section. The surviving kernel across all futures is: verified authority plus bonded recourse between machine actors across trust boundaries. Observation and measurement get absorbed; commitment and recourse do not.

Part IV — One Layer Deeper
The original thesis rested on "verification becomes more valuable than generation." Part III showed that law is half wrong: verification-as-analysis is itself intelligence, so abundant intelligence deflates it. The deeper structural law underneath:

As intelligence becomes abundant, the scarce factor shifts from knowing to committing. Information asymmetries collapse; commitment asymmetries remain, because a credible commitment requires capital at risk and enforceable recourse — things no amount of intelligence can synthesize.

And a second law that generates the market's shape:

Agents collapse transaction costs, so (per Coase) firm boundaries dissolve and the volume of arms-length transactions between strangers explodes — at machine speed. Institutions that establish who may act, on whose authority, with what recourse still run at human speed. The gap between machine-speed coordination and human-speed institutions is the opportunity.

I did, in fact, identify a product (audit + insurance) instead of the shift. The shift is: the economy is about to acquire hundreds of millions of new economic actors that have no identity, no legal personhood, no credit history, and no standing — and they will transact with strangers. The last time the economy absorbed a new actor type at scale (corporations, 19th century), it had to invent registries, agency law, audit, and liability insurance around them. That entire institutional stack must now be rebuilt for machine actors, at machine speed. Audit and insurance are two rooms in that building.

Part V — The Industry This Law Generates
Call the industry machine-speed institutions (the market will probably call it agentic trust infrastructure). Its layers, and where value pools:

Standards layer — delegation and identity protocols: the machine-readable "power of attorney" format, agent credentials, receipt schemas. Commoditized by design (foundations, consortia). Necessary; captures ~nothing. Do not try to own it — try to author it.
Infrastructure layer — agent identity registries and credential issuance binding agent → principal → scope of authority. Some value; heavy government/platform gravity.
Platform layer — the acceptance network. The gate on the counterparty side that answers, in milliseconds: is this agent who it claims, does it carry valid authority from a real principal for this action, and if this goes wrong, who pays and how is it resolved? This is the true Visa position — it sits inside the transaction, sees both sides, accumulates the loss data, and operates the dispute machinery. This is where long-term value accumulates, because it compounds three non-copyable assets at once: counterparty data, capital relationships, and two-sided network effects.
Application layer — vertical acceptance products (agent checkout, agent procurement, agent claims-filing, agent hiring). Healthy businesses; fragmented.
Financial layer — bonding and insurance capital. Commodity capital; it rents position from whoever holds the data. (The original thesis planned to be this layer via MGA — Part I showed that hands the moat to the carrier.)
Data layer — the cross-network corpus of agent fraud, authority abuse, and dispute outcomes. Critically, and unlike competence data: adversarial-loss data has a long half-life. Fraud patterns are about human adversaries and delegation structures, not model versions; they survive every model upgrade. This repairs the moat that KT-3 kills.
Regulatory layer — know-your-agent compliance. Whoever runs acceptance gets this pulled through for free.
Part VI — Twenty-Year Simulation (compressed to eras)
2026–2028. Agent commerce pilots; card networks extend protocols to agent-initiated payments; identity chaos — acceptors literally cannot distinguish an authorized purchasing agent from a scraper or a fraud bot. First public agent-fraud incidents. AI insurance premium stays small (consistent with KT-4 failing). Observability vendors all add "agent tracing" — the original wedge is commoditized roughly on schedule.

2028–2031. Agent-originated fraud becomes a top fraud vector; the acceptance side (banks, marketplaces, procurement platforms) starts bearing losses it can't attribute — and unlike agent vendors, acceptors already bear fraud loss and therefore want the infrastructure. Know-your-agent rules appear in financial regulation. Delegation-credential standards consolidate in a foundation. First bonded-delegation clauses appear in B2B contracts.

2031–2036. Firm boundaries visibly thin in procurement and back-office; agent-to-agent transactions between organizations become routine. Dispute volume forces automated arbitration — human-speed resolution is the bottleneck, exactly as the deeper law predicts. Model reliability is high; stochastic-error insurance is now cheap and boring (the original thesis's revenue base, deflated as predicted), while authority-and-fraud risk premiums grow with volume. Two or three acceptance networks emerge; at least one is a card network's extension.

2036–2046. Machine actors are ordinary economic participants. The surviving institutions, regardless of which companies won: agent identity registries, acceptance/clearing networks for agent actions, automated arbitration, and risk-capital markets for machine actors. Companies died; the functions persisted and concentrated.

What survives every branch of the simulation is the same kernel Part III found: verified authority plus bonded recourse at the moment a machine actor's action crosses a trust boundary. That is the true opportunity.

Part VII — The Rewritten Thesis
What the original got right (retained): trust is the post-abundance scarcity; risk transfer is the financial engine; neutrality is non-negotiable; disputes are the highest-value data; the business must grow mechanically with AI adoption.

What it got wrong (replaced):

Wrong side of the market. It instrumented the supply side (agent vendors), who are incentive-misaligned against measurement. The revised thesis serves the demand side — acceptors — who already bear the loss and beg for the gate. (Repairs Attacks 2 and 3; reframes KT-1: the test question becomes "what loss are acceptors eating from agent traffic today?", which has an observable, non-hypothetical answer.)
Wrong risk class. It underwrote stochastic model error, which declines with AI progress. The revised thesis underwrites adversarial and authority risk — impersonation, exceeded delegation, fraud — which grows with AI progress. This restores the required property that the business appreciates as AI improves, and repairs the moat (adversarial-loss data doesn't reset on model upgrades — answers KT-3).
Wrong network position. Receipts alongside the transaction clear nothing. The revised thesis sits inside the transaction at the acceptance moment, which is where Visa actually sat.
Revised thesis — the Agent Acceptance Network:

Build the infrastructure that lets any institution safely accept actions from AI agents it didn't build and doesn't control: verify the agent's identity and its delegated authority in-line, bond the action so recourse is guaranteed, and clear disputes at machine speed. Wedge: one acceptor category with acute, quantifiable agent-traffic losses today (payments-adjacent platforms or B2B procurement portals). Charge per accepted action plus a risk-based premium. The compounding assets are the cross-acceptor adversarial-loss corpus, the dispute-resolution machinery, and the two-sided network between principals who delegate and institutions that accept.

Revised core invariant:

No consequential action crosses a trust boundary without verified authority and bonded recourse.

Honest residual risks, with confidence:

Card networks are the natural owners of the payments slice and moved in 2025; the startup path runs through non-payment actions first (procurement, filings, account operations) and likely ends in partnership or acquisition by a network. High confidence this is the sharpest competitive risk.
Timing: if inter-organizational agent commerce stays trivial past ~2029, the acceptance network has no traffic. Medium confidence in the timeline; the simulation's 2028–2031 inflection is an informed hypothesis, not evidence.
The revised kill test: find five acceptors who can quantify in dollars the loss or blocked revenue from unidentifiable agent traffic today. If they can't, this thesis dies the same honest death the first one nearly did.
The one-paragraph judgment, revised: the first thesis correctly identified that trust, not capability, is the scarcity of the AI era — then built the wrong machine on that insight: it measured a shrinking risk from the side of the market that resents measurement. The falsification pass surfaced the deeper law: abundant intelligence makes knowing free and leaves committing scarce, while agents flood the economy with millions of new actors that have no identity, no authority trail, and no standing. The durable business is not the auditor of machine minds; it is the institution at the boundary — the gate that lets a stranger's agent act, because someone verified its authority and someone is bonded to make it right.
