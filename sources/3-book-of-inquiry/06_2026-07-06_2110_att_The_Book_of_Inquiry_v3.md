# The Book of Inquiry

## An Evolving Discipline for Thinking

> *"The purpose of this document is not to preserve conclusions. It is
> to preserve a way of searching that becomes better each time reality
> proves it wrong."*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Foreword

This is not a book of answers.

It is a book about how answers are earned.

Every chapter is expected to change.

If this document becomes static, it has failed.

**What this book actually is.** Not "critical thinking." Something more
specific: a manual for **allocating attention under uncertainty**.
Thinking is not free. Time, attention, energy, and emotional bandwidth
are limited. Every chapter here is ultimately about spending them well.

**Version note.** Third revision. The first version was a philosophy of
science. The second became a philosophy of problems. This one grows
legs: more problem types, levels of rigor, a chapter on misusing the
method, and companion artifacts meant for daily use. The book is the
constitution; the artifacts are the practice. They are kept separate on
purpose — the book is the thing you reread, the artifacts are the
things you use.

**Companion artifacts:**

-   *The Operating Card* — one page, consulted at the start of any
    non-trivial problem.
-   *The Inquiry Logs* — prediction log and failure log templates.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Core Purpose

The objective is not to:

-   invent startups,
-   predict the future,
-   produce elegant theories,
-   impress with abstraction.

The objective is to improve the process by which understanding itself is
created — and to spend that process only where it earns its cost.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Constitution

Reality outranks elegance.

Evidence outranks intuition.

Mechanisms outrank patterns.

Predictions outrank explanations.

Revision outranks consistency.

Unknowns are first-class citizens.

Confidence belongs to evidence, never to identity.

Confidence is graded, never granted. Theories do not get acquitted;
they accumulate trust in degrees, and can lose it in degrees.

Attention is the scarcest resource. Rigor applied to everything is
rigor applied to nothing.

Most of what I know arrived through other minds. Evaluating testimony
is not a special case of thinking — it is most of thinking.

The method itself can be misused. A discipline that cannot detect its
own abuse is a superstition with better formatting.

# The Spine

Before any method, three questions in sequence. Nearly every problem,
distilled far enough, reduces to them:

## 1. What am I actually trying to achieve?

Goals are mis-specified more often than solutions are wrong. Half of
all hard problems dissolve when the real objective is stated plainly.
Write the goal in one sentence. If the sentence contains an abstraction
("success," "quality," "growth"), replace it with something observable.

## 2. What is the binding constraint?

Most practical progress is limited by a small number of binding
constraints. Find the current one before optimizing anything else.

(In simple systems there is often exactly one bottleneck. In biology,
politics, relationships, and markets, constraints can interact, shift,
or hide inside feedback loops. The instruction survives either way:
name the constraint you believe is binding *now*, and treat that naming
as a hypothesis, not a fact.)

## 3. What is the cheapest observation that would discriminate between my candidate explanations?

Not the observation that would confirm the favorite — the one that
*splits* the candidates. Prefer tests that are cheap, fast, and
decisive over tests that are thorough, slow, and flattering.

Goal → constraint → discriminating test.

Everything else in this book is machinery for problems that survive
this sequence unresolved.

# Triage: Not Every Problem Deserves the Discipline

## First, classify the problem

Problems come in kinds, and the kinds have different shapes:

**Truth problems** — *What is actually the case?*
The full Discipline applies. Mechanisms, predictions, falsification.

**Decision problems** — *What should I do?*
These do not need a true theory; they need clarity about values,
probabilities, reversibility, and what I would do under each outcome.
The key question is not "what is true?" but "what would I need to
believe for each option to be correct, and how confident am I in each
belief?"

**Design problems** — *How should this thing work?*
There is no hidden truth to discover. There are tradeoffs to navigate
and constraints to satisfy. The key move is making tradeoffs explicit
instead of pretending an option dominates.

**Debugging problems** — *Why is this broken?*
A search problem over causes. The key move is binary-splitting the
search space: find the observation that eliminates half of the
remaining explanations, repeat.

**Coordination problems** — *How do multiple people act together
despite different incentives?*
Teams, companies, politics, families, institutions. These are not
solved by better analysis alone; they are solved by trust, incentives,
communication, commitment, and negotiation. The key move is making the
game explicit: who wants what, what does each party believe the others
will do, and what mechanism is missing — a commitment device, shared
information, an aligned incentive? Look for the missing mechanism
before looking for the villain. Most coordination failures have no
villain; they have a structure that makes good people act badly.

**Orientation problems** — *What matters — and who does choosing this
repeatedly make me?*
Career direction, habits, ambition, lifestyle, moral commitments, what
to optimize for at all. Evidence informs these; it cannot answer them.
They are the values question and the identity question fused, because
identity is values applied repeatedly over time. **Their method is
different in kind, not degree:** they are not resolved by
discriminating tests. They are clarified by honest articulation
(writing the competing values in plain words), by noticing what you
actually do rather than what you say, and by *small reversible
experiments in living* — trying a version of the life at low stakes and
attending honestly to what it felt like. Then revisiting. Reducing
these problems to optimization is not rigor; it is a category error
wearing rigor's clothes.

Misclassifying the problem type is the most expensive early error.
Treating a decision as a truth problem produces paralysis. Treating a
truth problem as a decision produces motivated reasoning. Treating a
coordination problem as a truth problem produces a correct analysis
that changes nothing. Treating an orientation problem as an
optimization produces a well-argued life you don't want.

**A ceiling on categories.** Six types is near the limit. Taxonomies
have costs too: when triage itself becomes a chore, it has failed its
own test. Resist the seventh category unless the failure journal
demands it.

## Second, budget the inquiry

Depth of inquiry should be proportional to:

    (stakes) × (irreversibility of being wrong)

High stakes, irreversible → full discipline.
High stakes, reversible → decide fast, instrument the outcome, revise.
Low stakes → heuristic and a shrug. Move on without guilt.

A bounded intelligence survives by deciding what not to think about.
This is that decision, made explicit.

## Third, choose the level of rigor

**Level 0 — Heuristic.** Decide immediately. Default, defaults, habit.
Most problems live here and should.

**Level 1 — Quick check.** One source, one test, one question to
someone who would know. Minutes.

**Level 2 — Discrimination.** Three competing explanations, one cheap
discriminating test. Hours to days. This is the workhorse level; most
problems that deserve real thought deserve exactly this much.

**Level 3 — Full loop.** The complete Discipline: mechanisms, dated
predictions with probabilities, pre-committed falsification, revision.
Days to weeks.

**Level 4 — Research program.** Prediction journal, failure journal,
external review, months-to-years horizon. Reserved for the questions
worth spending years on.

Name the level before starting. Escalate only when the current level
fails to resolve the problem *and* the stakes justify the next one.
De-escalate without shame.

## Fourth, set a stopping rule in advance

Before beginning, write down what "done" looks like:

-   *I stop when the expected value of further inquiry falls below its
    cost.*
-   *I stop when the leading explanation survives the two cheapest
    attempts to kill it.*
-   *I stop at [date], with whatever confidence I have then.*

Inquiry without a stopping rule is procrastination wearing a lab coat.

# The Discipline

For problems that triage as truth problems at Level 3 or above:

    Observe
     ↓
    Record
     ↓
    Question
     ↓
    Represent
     ↓
    Compress
     ↓
    Mechanism
     ↓
    Predict
     ↓
    Attempt to Falsify
     ↓
    Reality
     ↓
    Revise
     ↓
    Repeat

This loop is never finished.

**An honest correction about step one.** There is no theory-free
observation. What I notice is already selected by the concepts I carry
in; an anomaly is only anomalous relative to an expectation. The
practical instruction is not "observe without explaining" — it is:

> Observe while holding the current explanation loosely, and record
> especially what fails to fit it.

Write down the misfits verbatim, before interpretation, because
interpretation will quietly edit them.

# Generating Hypotheses

Testing is easy. Generation is the bottleneck. A pipeline that only
tests will starve. Deliberate moves for producing candidates:

**Steal from adjacent domains.** Ask: what problem in another field has
the same shape? How was it solved there? Most "original" mechanisms are
imports.

**Invert an assumption.** List everything the current framing takes as
given. Pick the most load-bearing one. Ask what the world looks like if
it is false.

**Interrogate the vocabulary.** The highest-value assumption is usually
hidden inside a word everyone uses without noticing. Define every noun
in the problem statement operationally. The one that resists definition
is often the door.

**Ask what would have to be true.** Instead of "is X the explanation?",
ask "if X were the explanation, what else would necessarily be true?"
Then look for that.

**Change the representation.** Draw it. Tabulate it. Tell it as a
story. Write it as an equation. Each representation makes different
structure visible and hides different structure. When stuck, the
problem is usually in the representation, not the reasoning.

**Ask what the experts next door take for granted.** Every field has
blind spots exactly where its neighboring field has instruments.

Generate at least three genuinely competing explanations before testing
any. One hypothesis is a conclusion in disguise. Two is a false
dichotomy waiting to happen. Three forces actual discrimination.

# Graded Confidence

Reality never delivers verdicts as clean as guilty or acquitted.
Replace the courtroom with a ledger.

**Assign rough numbers.** Not because the numbers are accurate, but
because "probably" hides multitudes. "70%" can be checked against
outcomes; "probably" cannot.

**Update proportionally.** Strong evidence moves confidence a lot; weak
evidence moves it a little. Evidence that would occur under *every*
candidate explanation moves it not at all — however vivid it feels.

**Respect base rates.** Before asking "does the evidence fit my
theory?", ask "how common is this kind of thing in general?" Most
exciting explanations lose to boring priors.

**Track calibration.** Periodically check: of the things I marked 80%,
did roughly 80% come true? A mind can be intelligent and badly
calibrated. Calibration, unlike intelligence, is trainable — but only
with the receipts.

**When a prediction fails, the failure is ambiguous.** A failed
prediction never announces *which* assumption failed: the theory, the
measurement, the auxiliary assumptions, or the test itself. Do not
abandon a theory over one failure; do not rescue it endlessly either.
The discipline is to write down, *in advance*, which failures would be
fatal and which would be survivable. Advance commitments are the only
defense against post-hoc rescue.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Evaluating What Others Claim

Almost everything I know arrived through testimony — people, papers,
institutions, tools. I will personally verify almost none of it. This
is not a weakness to eliminate; it is the condition of being a bounded
mind. The skill is auditing the pipeline, not replacing it.

For any claim I cannot check directly, ask:

**What are the incentives of the source?** Not "are they honest?" but
"what would they say if they were wrong, and does that differ from what
they are saying?"

**Does the source's field have error-correcting feedback?** Fields
where predictions visibly fail (engineering, markets, medicine
eventually) self-correct. Fields where failure is deniable accumulate
confident error. Weight testimony by the field's feedback loops, not
its prestige.

**Is the claim upstream or downstream of selection?** Ask what I am
*not* seeing: the failed studies, the quiet counterexamples, the people
who tried this and stopped. Survivorship is the default filter on
everything that reaches me.

**Can the source describe the strongest opposing case?** Someone who
cannot state the best argument against their position does not hold a
position; they hold an allegiance.

**How does the claim behave at the edges?** Push it to extremes. Claims
that are true only in the comfortable middle are usually descriptions,
not mechanisms.

# Misuse of the Discipline

Every powerful method becomes a trap in the hands of the person it was
built to protect. This book will be misused, by its own author, in
predictable ways. Name them now so they can be recognized later:

**Inquiry as avoidance of action.** Running Level 3 on a problem whose
answer is already known and merely unwelcome. The tell: the analysis
keeps growing while the situation stays the same.

**Skepticism as avoidance of commitment.** "The evidence isn't
conclusive yet" — said for the fourth year in a row, about a decision
that was reversible all along. Doubt can be a fortress. Fortresses are
places people hide.

**Evidence as a hiding place for fear.** Demanding proof for the path
you are afraid of and accepting anecdotes for the path you are not.
Check: is the standard of evidence the same in both directions?

**Abstraction as avoidance of emotion.** Converting a grief, a
conflict, or a longing into a "system" with "constraints" because
systems don't hurt. Some problems must be felt before they can be
thought. The framework is not a substitute for the conversation, the
apology, or the mourning.

**Prediction as avoidance of responsibility.** "I assigned it 40%" is
not absolution when you chose it anyway. Probabilities inform choices;
they do not make them, and they do not own their consequences.

**Intelligence as rationalization.** The strongest minds build the
strongest cases for what they already wanted. The more articulate the
justification, the more suspicious you should be that the conclusion
came first. The diagnostic: did the analysis ever, even once, move you
*away* from your initial preference? A method that only ever confirms
is not a method. It is a mirror.

The common structure of all six: the Discipline used as a *shield*
rather than a *search*. The test is always the same — is this activity
still exposed to reality, or has it become a way of staying safe from
it?

# Principles Worth Remembering

## On Reality

> Reality has no obligation to be understandable.

> Nature is the only reviewer that cannot be persuaded.

> Every theory is a compression algorithm competing against reality.

> The map should continually lose to the territory — and improve because
> of it.

## On Discovery

> Most discoveries begin where the current theory is most embarrassed.

> The highest-value assumption is usually hidden inside the vocabulary.

> A pattern without a mechanism is a question pretending to be an
> answer.

> The best anomaly is one that refuses to disappear.

## On Intelligence

> Intelligence is finite attention allocated within an effectively
> infinite search space.

> The rarest intelligence is knowing where attention should go next.

> Every finite intelligence survives by deciding what not to think
> about.

> Better reasoning often begins with better problem selection.

## On Knowledge

> Unknowns are assets.

> Confidence should grow only as alternatives disappear.

> Every answer should leave behind a better question.

> A theory earns trust by surviving reality — not by sounding profound.

## On Theories

Compression without prediction is philosophy.

Prediction without mechanism is coincidence.

Mechanism without evidence is speculation.

Evidence without revision is dogma.

## On Problems

A problem correctly classified is half solved.

A goal stated observably is half achieved.

A constraint correctly named is half removed.

## On the Method Itself

A discipline that cannot be misused has no power.

A discipline whose misuse cannot be detected has no honesty.

# Research Operating System

For any domain, after triage:

1.  Classify the problem type; choose the level of rigor; budget the
    inquiry.
2.  State the goal observably. Name the binding constraint (as a
    hypothesis).
3.  Observe holding explanations loosely; record misfits verbatim.
4.  Grade evidence.
5.  Define ontology; operationalize every noun.
6.  Identify assumptions; rank by load-bearing weight.
7.  Hunt anomalies.
8.  Change representation.
9.  Generate at least three competing explanations.
10. Demand mechanisms.
11. Apply constraints.
12. Make dated predictions with rough probabilities; pre-commit to what
    would count as fatal failure.
13. Design the cheapest discriminating test.
14. Revise proportionally to the strength of evidence.
15. Check the stopping rule.
16. Check for misuse: is this still a search, or has it become a
    shield?

# Validation Framework

## Historical Rediscovery

Attempt to rediscover major breakthroughs using only information
available before they happened.

Examples: Darwin, Einstein, Shannon, Wegener, Turing, Semmelweis,
Marshall.

Evaluate:

-   anomaly identification
-   assumption discovery
-   mechanism quality
-   prediction quality
-   false-positive rate

## Cross-Domain Test

Apply the same methodology to physics, biology, economics, software,
medicine, psychology, law, history, business.

If the process changes fundamentally, understand why. (Expect it to
change at the triage layer — the problem *types* differ across domains
even when the truth-seeking core does not.)

## Prediction Test

Maintain a prediction journal (see *The Inquiry Logs*). Each prediction
must be:

-   Specific
-   Observable
-   Dated
-   Falsifiable
-   Accompanied by a rough probability

**Match the review cadence to the prediction's horizon.** Research
predictions: review at 6, 12, 36 months. Daily-life and work
predictions: review weekly. Calibration is trained by fast feedback;
a journal reviewed yearly trains nothing.

## Failure Journal

Every failed prediction records (template in *The Inquiry Logs*):

-   wrong assumption
-   wrong evidence
-   wrong mechanism
-   wrong representation
-   missing constraint
-   wrong problem classification
-   whether the failure was pre-committed as fatal, or is being
    rationalized now
-   lesson learned

Failure is data. Rationalized failure is corrupted data.

## Compression Test

Prefer theories that:

-   explain more,
-   assume less,
-   predict better,
-   survive falsification,
-   generalize across domains.

# Worked Examples

*One per problem type that has a distinct method — kept short on
purpose. The point is not coverage of life's domains; a health question
and a historical claim are both truth problems and need no separate
demonstration. The point is that the method genuinely changes with the
problem type.*

## 1. Debugging (Level 2)

**Situation.** A small web product's sign-ups have dropped 30% over two
months.

**Triage.** Debugging problem. Stakes high; reversibility unknown,
which raises urgency. Level 2. Stopping rule: *one explanation survives
the two cheapest attempts to kill it, or five days, whichever comes
first.*

**Spine.** Goal: restore sign-up rate to baseline. Constraint
hypothesis: unknown — finding it *is* the problem. Cheapest
discriminating observation: where in the funnel did the drop occur?

**Observation, held loosely.** Visits flat; the drop sits between
"landing page view" and "form submit." Misfit recorded verbatim: the
drop began ten days *before* the last release — which quietly kills the
favorite explanation ("the new release broke something") before anyone
gets attached to it.

**Three competing explanations.** (a) A competitor's pricing change is
diverting intent. (b) A browser update broke the form for a segment.
(c) A traffic-source shift: same volume, lower-intent visitors.

**Mechanisms.** (a) predicts the drop concentrates in price-sensitive
segments; (b) in one browser/version; (c) predicts referrer mix changed
while per-source conversion stayed flat.

**Discriminating test.** One query: conversion segmented by browser and
referrer. Cost: an hour.

**Reality.** Per-referrer conversion flat; referrer mix shifted — a
high-converting channel lost reach. (c) survives; (a) and (b) die
cheaply.

**Prediction.** "Restoring effort on the lost channel recovers sign-ups
to within 10% of baseline within three weeks — 75%."

Total cost: two days. The full Discipline was never needed. That is the
system working — ceremony matched to problem.

## 2. Decision (Level 2)

**Situation.** A job offer: 30% more pay at a larger company, versus
staying at a startup with equity and broader responsibility.

**Triage.** Decision problem — there is no hidden truth to discover
about which job "is better." Stakes high; reversibility *moderate and
underrated* (people return from wrong jobs constantly). Level 2.
Stopping rule: two weeks, then decide with whatever clarity exists.

**The move that matters.** Not more research — *belief mapping*. For
each option, write what would have to be true for it to be the right
choice:

-   The offer is right if: the startup's next funding round is unlikely
    (~40%?), the larger company's role has real scope (unknown —
    checkable), and the money materially changes life plans (checkable
    against actual budget, not vibes).
-   Staying is right if: the equity has a plausible path to meaning
    (base rate for startups at this stage: low — respect it), and the
    broader responsibility compounds into skills the market pays for
    (strong evidence from the last two years).

**Cheapest discriminating observations.** Ask the larger company to
describe the last three projects someone in this role owned (tests
"real scope" in one conversation). Ask the startup's CEO directly about
runway (tests the funding belief).

**The decision rule, set in advance.** "If the role's scope is real and
runway is under 12 months, take the offer. Otherwise stay one more
funding cycle." Then the answers decide — not the mood on decision day.

Note what was *not* done: no attempt to predict the future of either
company at Level 4. The decision needed calibrated beliefs and a
pre-committed rule, not a forecast.

## 3. Coordination (Level 2)

**Situation.** Engineering and sales at a 40-person company are in open
conflict. Sales promises custom features to close deals; engineering
misses deadlines because the roadmap keeps changing; each side calls
the other the problem.

**Triage.** Coordination problem. Treating it as a truth problem
("which team is right?") would produce a correct analysis that changes
nothing. Level 2.

**Make the game explicit.** Sales is paid on closed revenue this
quarter; promising features costs them nothing and wins deals.
Engineering is judged on delivery against a roadmap they don't control.
Each side is responding rationally to its own incentives. *There is no
villain; there is a structure.*

**Find the missing mechanism.** The system lacks a commitment device:
nothing makes a sales promise costly to the person making it. Candidate
mechanisms: (a) custom-feature promises require engineering sign-off
before the contract is sent; (b) a fixed monthly capacity budget for
custom work that sales allocates themselves — making the tradeoff
*theirs*; (c) part of sales compensation tied to delivered (not
promised) features.

**Cheapest test.** Mechanism (b) is reversible and needs no comp
changes: run it for one quarter. Prediction: escalations drop by half
because the conflict moves from between-teams to within sales's own
budget — where tradeoffs belong. 65%.

Note the shape: the inquiry produced a *mechanism to install*, not a
conclusion about who was right. That is what coordination problems
yield when solved.

## 4. Orientation (different method entirely)

**Situation.** Five years into a well-paying technical career, a
persistent pull toward teaching. The spreadsheet has been made four
times. It always says stay. The pull persists.

**Triage.** Orientation problem. The recurring spreadsheet is the tell:
a values question is being fed into an optimization and returning
answers that don't resolve anything — because it is being asked to
answer what it can only inform. The persistence of the pull *after*
four analyses is itself the most important datum on the page.

**Articulation instead of analysis.** Write the competing values in
plain words, without weights: "I value building things that outlast
me." "I value seeing the effect of my work on a particular face."
"I fear being ordinary more than I fear being unhappy." That last
sentence took three drafts to admit. It reorganizes everything above
it.

**Watch behavior, not statements.** What do the last two years of
choices reveal? Every discretionary hour went to mentoring juniors, not
to side projects. The revealed preference has been voting for years.

**A small reversible experiment in living.** Not resignation — one
evening course taught at a local institute for a semester. Attend
honestly to what it feels like: not "was it optimal," but "did the
weeks with it feel more like mine than the weeks without it."

**Revisit.** In six months, with the experiment's texture in hand — not
to close the question forever, but to take the next reversible step.
Orientation problems are not solved. They are *walked*.

Note what would have been misuse here: running the spreadsheet a fifth
time (abstraction as avoidance of emotion; inquiry as avoidance of
action). The discipline's contribution was recognizing which problem
type this was — and getting out of the way.

# Epistemic Warnings

Beware:

-   elegant narratives
-   confirmation bias
-   authority bias
-   extrapolating trends indefinitely
-   confusing possibility with inevitability
-   mistaking explanation for prediction
-   stopping because a story feels satisfying
-   rigor as procrastination — running the full loop on a problem that
    needed an afternoon
-   the single-hypothesis trap — testing one idea is confirming one
    idea
-   post-hoc rescue — deciding after the failure which assumptions were
    "auxiliary"
-   prestige as evidence — weighting testimony by the speaker's status
    instead of their field's feedback loops
-   the method as shield — any use of this book that reduces exposure
    to reality instead of increasing it (see *Misuse of the
    Discipline*)

# The Practice

The hard part of this discipline is not intellectual. It is sitting
with an unresolved anomaly for months without forcing a resolution. It
is watching a prediction you were proud of fail in public. It is
running the boring loop on the two-hundredth day exactly as on the
second.

Three honest admissions:

**Motivation decays; systems persist.** Do not rely on caring. Rely on
cadence — a fixed, small, recurring review that happens whether or not
it feels important that day. Ten minutes weekly beats a heroic monthly
audit that stops after month three.

**Identity is the enemy of revision.** The moment a theory becomes
"mine," evidence against it becomes evidence against me, and the whole
machinery silently inverts. The defense is procedural, not moral: state
theories in the third person, pre-commit to fatal tests, and let the
journal — not the mood — decide what survived.

**Discomfort is the signal working.** The feeling of an anomaly that
refuses to disappear is unpleasant by design; it is the sensation of a
map failing. Train the response: discomfort → record it → schedule it.
Not discomfort → explain it away.

If this document is consulted often and the journals stay empty, the
document has become decoration. The journals are the practice. This is
just the manual.

# The Amendment Rule

The Foreword promises that every chapter is expected to change. A
promise without a mechanism is a mood. Here is the mechanism:

**A chapter is amended when:**

1.  The failure journal contradicts it twice — two independent failures
    traceable to following the chapter's guidance; or
2.  A real problem, worked honestly, could not be served by the
    existing categories or methods; or
3.  A worked example reveals that the book's instruction and the
    successful practice diverged.

**A chapter is never amended because:**

-   a smarter-sounding framing appeared;
-   feedback was articulate (articulate feedback is a hypothesis, not
    evidence — including the feedback that produced this version);
-   the current wording became boring to its author.

**Every amendment is logged:** date, chapter, cause (which of the three
triggers), and what the previous version got wrong. The book keeps its
own failure journal. A constitution that revises itself on eloquence
alone is not evolving. It is drifting.

# The Ultimate Objective

Do not build theories.

Build better ways of evaluating theories.

Do not search for certainty.

Search for methods that reduce uncertainty — and know what a unit of
uncertainty-reduction costs, so you can stop paying when the price
exceeds the worth.

The greatest discovery may not be a new explanation of reality.

It may be a better process for discovering explanations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Closing Thought

The quality of a mind is determined less by the answers it reaches than
by the process it is willing to subject those answers to — and by its
honesty about which problems deserve that process at all.
