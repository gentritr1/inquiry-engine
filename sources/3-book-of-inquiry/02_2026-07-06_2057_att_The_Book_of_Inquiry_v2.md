# The Book of Inquiry

## An Evolving Discipline for Thinking

> *"The purpose of this document is not to preserve conclusions. It is
> to preserve a way of searching that becomes better each time reality
> proves it wrong."*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Foreword

This is not a book of answers.

It is a book about how answers are earned.

Every chapter is expected to change.

If this document becomes static, it has failed.

**Version note.** This is the second revision. The first version was a
philosophy of science. This version tries to be a philosophy of
problems. The difference matters: most problems are not scientific
questions, and a discipline that treats every problem like Darwin's
problem will waste its owner's life on rigor misapplied.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Core Purpose

The objective is not to:

-   invent startups,
-   predict the future,
-   produce elegant theories,
-   impress with abstraction.

The objective is to improve the process by which understanding itself is
created — and to spend that process only where it earns its cost.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Constitution

Reality outranks elegance.

Evidence outranks intuition.

Mechanisms outrank patterns.

Predictions outrank explanations.

Revision outranks consistency.

Unknowns are first-class citizens.

Confidence belongs to evidence, never to identity.

Confidence is graded, never granted. Theories do not get acquitted;
they accumulate trust in degrees, and can lose it in degrees.

Attention is the scarcest resource. Rigor applied to everything is
rigor applied to nothing.

Most of what I know arrived through other minds. Evaluating testimony
is not a special case of thinking — it is most of thinking.

# The Spine

Before any method, three questions in sequence. Nearly every problem,
distilled far enough, reduces to them:

## 1. What am I actually trying to achieve?

Goals are mis-specified more often than solutions are wrong. Half of
all hard problems dissolve when the real objective is stated plainly.
Write the goal in one sentence. If the sentence contains an abstraction
("success," "quality," "growth"), replace it with something observable.

## 2. What is the binding constraint?

Every system is dominated by one bottleneck at a time. Effort applied
anywhere else is decoration. Name the constraint before proposing
anything. If you cannot name it, that is the first inquiry.

## 3. What is the cheapest observation that would discriminate between my candidate explanations?

Not the observation that would confirm the favorite — the one that
*splits* the candidates. Prefer tests that are cheap, fast, and
decisive over tests that are thorough, slow, and flattering.

Goal → constraint → discriminating test.

Everything else in this book is machinery for problems that survive
this sequence unresolved.

# Triage: Not Every Problem Deserves the Discipline

## First, classify the problem

Problems come in kinds, and the kinds have different shapes:

**Truth problems** — *What is actually the case?*
The full Discipline applies. Mechanisms, predictions, falsification.

**Decision problems** — *What should I do?*
These do not need a true theory; they need clarity about values,
probabilities, reversibility, and what I would do under each outcome.
The key question is not "what is true?" but "what would I need to
believe for each option to be correct, and how confident am I in each
belief?"

**Design problems** — *How should this thing work?*
There is no hidden truth to discover. There are tradeoffs to navigate
and constraints to satisfy. The key move is making tradeoffs explicit
instead of pretending an option dominates.

**Debugging problems** — *Why is this broken?*
A search problem over causes. The key move is binary-splitting the
search space: find the observation that eliminates half of the
remaining explanations, repeat.

Misclassifying the problem type is the most expensive early error.
Treating a decision as a truth problem produces paralysis. Treating a
truth problem as a decision produces motivated reasoning.

## Second, budget the inquiry

Depth of inquiry should be proportional to:

    (stakes) × (irreversibility of being wrong)

High stakes, irreversible → full discipline.
High stakes, reversible → decide fast, instrument the outcome, revise.
Low stakes → heuristic and a shrug. Move on without guilt.

A bounded intelligence survives by deciding what not to think about.
This is that decision, made explicit.

## Third, set a stopping rule in advance

Before beginning, write down what "done" looks like:

-   *I stop when the expected value of further inquiry falls below its
    cost.*
-   *I stop when the leading explanation survives the two cheapest
    attempts to kill it.*
-   *I stop at [date], with whatever confidence I have then.*

Inquiry without a stopping rule is procrastination wearing a lab coat.

# The Discipline

For problems that triage as truth problems with real stakes:

    Observe
     ↓
    Record
     ↓
    Question
     ↓
    Represent
     ↓
    Compress
     ↓
    Mechanism
     ↓
    Predict
     ↓
    Attempt to Falsify
     ↓
    Reality
     ↓
    Revise
     ↓
    Repeat

This loop is never finished.

**An honest correction about step one.** There is no theory-free
observation. What I notice is already selected by the concepts I carry
in; an anomaly is only anomalous relative to an expectation. The
practical instruction is not "observe without explaining" — it is:

> Observe while holding the current explanation loosely, and record
> especially what fails to fit it.

Write down the misfits verbatim, before interpretation, because
interpretation will quietly edit them.

# Generating Hypotheses

Testing is easy. Generation is the bottleneck. A pipeline that only
tests will starve. Deliberate moves for producing candidates:

**Steal from adjacent domains.** Ask: what problem in another field has
the same shape? How was it solved there? Most "original" mechanisms are
imports.

**Invert an assumption.** List everything the current framing takes as
given. Pick the most load-bearing one. Ask what the world looks like if
it is false.

**Interrogate the vocabulary.** The highest-value assumption is usually
hidden inside a word everyone uses without noticing. Define every noun
in the problem statement operationally. The one that resists definition
is often the door.

**Ask what would have to be true.** Instead of "is X the explanation?",
ask "if X were the explanation, what else would necessarily be true?"
Then look for that.

**Change the representation.** Draw it. Tabulate it. Tell it as a
story. Write it as an equation. Each representation makes different
structure visible and hides different structure. When stuck, the
problem is usually in the representation, not the reasoning.

**Ask what the experts next door take for granted.** Every field has
blind spots exactly where its neighboring field has instruments.

Generate at least three genuinely competing explanations before testing
any. One hypothesis is a conclusion in disguise. Two is a false
dichotomy waiting to happen. Three forces actual discrimination.

# Graded Confidence

The old constitution said theories begin guilty and reality grants
acquittal. Reality never delivers verdicts that clean. Replace the
courtroom with a ledger.

**Assign rough numbers.** Not because the numbers are accurate, but
because "probably" hides multitudes. "70%" can be checked against
outcomes; "probably" cannot.

**Update proportionally.** Strong evidence moves confidence a lot; weak
evidence moves it a little. Evidence that would occur under *every*
candidate explanation moves it not at all — however vivid it feels.

**Respect base rates.** Before asking "does the evidence fit my
theory?", ask "how common is this kind of thing in general?" Most
exciting explanations lose to boring priors.

**Track calibration.** Periodically check: of the things I marked 80%,
did roughly 80% come true? A mind can be intelligent and badly
calibrated. Calibration, unlike intelligence, is trainable — but only
with the receipts.

**When a prediction fails, the failure is ambiguous.** A failed
prediction never announces *which* assumption failed: the theory, the
measurement, the auxiliary assumptions, or the test itself. Do not
abandon a theory over one failure; do not rescue it endlessly either.
The discipline is to write down, *in advance*, which failures would be
fatal and which would be survivable. Advance commitments are the only
defense against post-hoc rescue.

# Evaluating What Others Claim

Almost everything I know arrived through testimony — people, papers,
institutions, tools. I will personally verify almost none of it. This
is not a weakness to eliminate; it is the condition of being a bounded
mind. The skill is auditing the pipeline, not replacing it.

For any claim I cannot check directly, ask:

**What are the incentives of the source?** Not "are they honest?" but
"what would they say if they were wrong, and does that differ from what
they are saying?"

**Does the source's field have error-correcting feedback?** Fields
where predictions visibly fail (engineering, markets, medicine
eventually) self-correct. Fields where failure is deniable accumulate
confident error. Weight testimony by the field's feedback loops, not
its prestige.

**Is the claim upstream or downstream of selection?** Ask what I am
*not* seeing: the failed studies, the quiet counterexamples, the people
who tried this and stopped. Survivorship is the default filter on
everything that reaches me.

**Can the source describe the strongest opposing case?** Someone who
cannot state the best argument against their position does not hold a
position; they hold an allegiance.

**How does the claim behave at the edges?** Push it to extremes. Claims
that are true only in the comfortable middle are usually descriptions,
not mechanisms.

# Principles Worth Remembering

## On Reality

> Reality has no obligation to be understandable.

> Nature is the only reviewer that cannot be persuaded.

> Every theory is a compression algorithm competing against reality.

> The map should continually lose to the territory — and improve because
> of it.

## On Discovery

> Most discoveries begin where the current theory is most embarrassed.

> The highest-value assumption is usually hidden inside the vocabulary.

> A pattern without a mechanism is a question pretending to be an
> answer.

> The best anomaly is one that refuses to disappear.

## On Intelligence

> Intelligence is finite attention allocated within an effectively
> infinite search space.

> The rarest intelligence is knowing where attention should go next.

> Every finite intelligence survives by deciding what not to think
> about.

> Better reasoning often begins with better problem selection.

## On Knowledge

> Unknowns are assets.

> Confidence should grow only as alternatives disappear.

> Every answer should leave behind a better question.

> A theory earns trust by surviving reality — not by sounding profound.

## On Theories

Compression without prediction is philosophy.

Prediction without mechanism is coincidence.

Mechanism without evidence is speculation.

Evidence without revision is dogma.

## On Problems

A problem correctly classified is half solved.

A goal stated observably is half achieved.

A constraint correctly named is half removed.

# Research Operating System

For any domain, after triage:

1.  Classify the problem type and budget the inquiry.
2.  State the goal observably. Name the binding constraint.
3.  Observe holding explanations loosely; record misfits verbatim.
4.  Grade evidence.
5.  Define ontology; operationalize every noun.
6.  Identify assumptions; rank by load-bearing weight.
7.  Hunt anomalies.
8.  Change representation.
9.  Generate at least three competing explanations.
10. Demand mechanisms.
11. Apply constraints.
12. Make dated predictions with rough probabilities; pre-commit to what
    would count as fatal failure.
13. Design the cheapest discriminating test.
14. Revise proportionally to the strength of evidence.
15. Check the stopping rule.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Validation Framework

## Historical Rediscovery

Attempt to rediscover major breakthroughs using only information
available before they happened.

Examples: Darwin, Einstein, Shannon, Wegener, Turing, Semmelweis,
Marshall.

Evaluate:

-   anomaly identification
-   assumption discovery
-   mechanism quality
-   prediction quality
-   false-positive rate

## Cross-Domain Test

Apply the same methodology to physics, biology, economics, software,
medicine, psychology, law, history, business.

If the process changes fundamentally, understand why. (Expect it to
change at the triage layer — the problem *types* differ across domains
even when the truth-seeking core does not.)

## Prediction Test

Maintain a prediction journal. Each prediction must be:

-   Specific
-   Observable
-   Dated
-   Falsifiable
-   Accompanied by a rough probability

**Match the review cadence to the prediction's horizon.** Research
predictions: review at 6, 12, 36 months. Daily-life and work
predictions: review weekly. Calibration is trained by fast feedback;
a journal reviewed yearly trains nothing.

## Failure Journal

Every failed prediction records:

-   wrong assumption
-   wrong evidence
-   wrong mechanism
-   wrong representation
-   missing constraint
-   *(added)* wrong problem classification
-   *(added)* whether the failure was pre-committed as fatal, or is
    being rationalized now
-   lesson learned

Failure is data. Rationalized failure is corrupted data.

## Compression Test

Prefer theories that:

-   explain more,
-   assume less,
-   predict better,
-   survive falsification,
-   generalize across domains.

# A Worked Example

*A discipline that is never demonstrated is a decoration. One real pass,
start to finish, at modest scale.*

**Situation.** A small web product's sign-ups have dropped 30% over two
months.

**Triage.** This is a *debugging problem*, not a truth problem — no
grand theory needed. Stakes: high. Reversibility: unknown, which itself
raises urgency. Budget: days, not months. Stopping rule: *stop when one
explanation survives the two cheapest attempts to kill it, or in five
days, whichever comes first.*

**Spine.** Goal, observably: restore sign-up rate to the prior
baseline. Binding constraint, candidate: unknown — finding it *is* the
problem. Cheapest discriminating observation: where in the funnel did
the drop occur?

**Observation, held loosely.** Funnel data shows visits are flat;
the drop is between "landing page view" and "form submit." Misfit
recorded verbatim: the drop began ten days *before* the last product
release — which quietly kills the favorite explanation ("our new
release broke something") before anyone gets attached to it.

**Three competing explanations.**
(a) A pricing change by a competitor is diverting intent.
(b) A browser update broke the form for some segment.
(c) A traffic-source shift: same volume, lower-intent visitors.

**Mechanisms demanded.** (a) predicts the drop concentrates in
price-sensitive segments; (b) predicts it concentrates in one
browser/version; (c) predicts referrer mix changed and per-source
conversion is actually flat.

**Cheapest discriminating test.** One query: conversion segmented by
browser and by referrer. Cost: an hour.

**Reality.** Conversion per referrer is flat; the referrer mix shifted —
a channel that historically converted well lost reach. Explanation (c)
survives; (a) and (b) die cheaply.

**Prediction, dated, with probability.** "If we restore spend/effort on
the lost channel, sign-ups recover to within 10% of baseline within
three weeks — 75%."

**Revision hook.** If it fails: the wrong assumption was probably that
channel intent is stable over time — record it in the failure journal
and re-run generation.

Total cost: two days. The full Discipline was never needed; triage,
spine, three candidates, one discriminating query. *That* is the system
working — not the loop running at maximum ceremony, but the ceremony
matching the problem.

# Epistemic Warnings

Beware:

-   elegant narratives
-   confirmation bias
-   authority bias
-   extrapolating trends indefinitely
-   confusing possibility with inevitability
-   mistaking explanation for prediction
-   stopping because a story feels satisfying
-   *(added)* rigor as procrastination — running the full loop on a
    problem that needed an afternoon
-   *(added)* the single-hypothesis trap — testing one idea is
    confirming one idea
-   *(added)* post-hoc rescue — deciding after the failure which
    assumptions were "auxiliary"
-   *(added)* prestige as evidence — weighting testimony by the
    speaker's status instead of their field's feedback loops

# The Practice

The hard part of this discipline is not intellectual. It is sitting
with an unresolved anomaly for months without forcing a resolution. It
is watching a prediction you were proud of fail in public. It is
running the boring loop on the two-hundredth day exactly as on the
second.

Three honest admissions:

**Motivation decays; systems persist.** Do not rely on caring. Rely on
cadence — a fixed, small, recurring review that happens whether or not
it feels important that day. Ten minutes weekly beats a heroic monthly
audit that stops after month three.

**Identity is the enemy of revision.** The moment a theory becomes
"mine," evidence against it becomes evidence against me, and the whole
machinery silently inverts. The defense is procedural, not moral: state
theories in the third person, pre-commit to fatal tests, and let the
journal — not the mood — decide what survived.

**Discomfort is the signal working.** The feeling of an anomaly that
refuses to disappear is unpleasant by design; it is the sensation of a
map failing. Train the response: discomfort → record it → schedule it.
Not discomfort → explain it away.

If this document is consulted often and the journals stay empty, the
document has become decoration. The journals are the practice. This is
just the manual.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The Ultimate Objective

Do not build theories.

Build better ways of evaluating theories.

Do not search for certainty.

Search for methods that reduce uncertainty — and know what a unit of
uncertainty-reduction costs, so you can stop paying when the price
exceeds the worth.

The greatest discovery may not be a new explanation of reality.

It may be a better process for discovering explanations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Closing Thought

The quality of a mind is determined less by the answers it reaches than
by the process it is willing to subject those answers to — and by its
honesty about which problems deserve that process at all.
